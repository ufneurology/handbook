Myasthenia Gravis Exacerbation

Author

Luis Silva

Modified

May 26, 2026

1 Epidemiology

  • 2:3 M:F ratio, bimodal peaks (females in 20s, males in 50s)
  • 20% bulbar-only
  • Incidence ~2 per 100,000 per year

2 Presentation

  • Facial diparesis, ptosis, neck extensor weakness, nasal voice, hypotonic/weak voice
  • May be precipitated by: medication non-compliance, excessive activity, infection, or drugs
  • Different from “cholinergic crisis” – weakness, fasciculations, increased sweating/salivation, and miosis

3 Workup & Admission (similar respiratory concerns as acute GBS)

  1. Assess respiratory status with Vital Capacity (VC) and Negative Inspiratory Force (NIF) — follow q2h if worrisome.

    Important

    Pulse oximetry and ABG lag behind hypoventilation!

  2. Non-invasive and Invansive ventiation

Consider non-ventilation (BIPAP) before intubation, as it can prevent intubation unless patient is hypercarbic.

Intubation thresholds (20/30/40 rule):

  • VC <20 mL/kg (or <1 L): Bedside VC Estimate: Count aloud in one breath × 100 cc (e.g., counts to 25 = 2.5 L)

  • MIP <30 cmH₂O

  • MEP <40 cmH₂O

  • OR there is a Reduction in VC, MIP, MEP >30%

Warning

Facial weakness may cause underestimation due to poor seal

Intubation Medications

  • Etomidate 0.2–0.3 mg/kg

  • Non-depolarizing paralytic if needed (e.g., vecuronium)

Extubation criteria:

  • Rapid Shallow Breathing Index (RSBI) <100

  • FVC >15 mL/kg

  • NIF >–30 cmH₂O

  • PEF >40 cmH₂O

  1. If new diagnosis:

    • Acetylcholine receptor antibodies (binding, blocking, modulating)
    • MuSK antibody (especially young females)
    • EMG/NCS with repetitive stimulation ± single fiber EMG
    • Consider edrophonium (Tensilon) test
    • Thyroid function tests
    • Chest CT to rule out thymoma (± anti-striated muscle Ab)

  2. Acute treatment:

    • Plasma exchange (PLEX) and/or IVIG + initiate acetylcholinesterase inhibitors

    • Start or continue steroids?
      Steroids ≥ 40mg/d may acutely worsen symptoms, so consider starting at lower dose and ramp in tandem with PLEX/IVIG, or continue current dose if >40mg + ramp after PLEX/IVIG

    • Hold pyridostigmine if the patient is intubated/ventilated (increases secretions). Restart at ½ dose approximately 24 hours before planned extubation.

4 Acute & Chronic Treatment

4.1 Acute Treatments

Treatment Dosing Time to Onset Adverse Effects
Plasma Exchange 1–1.5× plasma volume × 5 treatments qd or qod 2–3 treatments Coagulopathy, line infection, arrhythmia, shock
IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 4–5 days ~5 days Aseptic meningitis, fluid overload

4.2 Chronic / Maintenance Treatments

Treatment Dosing Time to Onset Adverse Effects
Pyridostigmine (Mestinon) 30–120 mg q3–6h (max 120 mg q3h) Immediate GI upset, cramps, pulmonary edema
Prednisone 60–80 mg/day with slow taper 2–4 weeks Osteoporosis, weight gain, hyperglycemia
Azathioprine (Imuran) 1–3 mg/kg/day 3–9 months Leukopenia, hepatotoxicity
Mycophenolate (Cellcept) 1000 mg BID Few months Anemia, leukopenia, diarrhea

4.3 Symptomatic Treatment with Pyridostigmine (Mestinon)

Initiation & Titration:

Start 30 mg PO TID, increase to 60–120 mg every 4–6 hours (maintenance dose). Maximum: 120 mg every 3 hours

Additional Notes:

  • Long-acting Mestinon (Timespan) — useful at bedtime for nocturnal or early-morning weakness

  • IV Mestinon dose = 1/30th of the oral dose

  • Use Robinul (glycopyrrolate) for excessive GI cholinergic side effects

  • Monitor closely for ileus or megacolon

4.4 Long-Term Immunotherapy

  • Prednisone (first-line)
  • Consider steroid-sparing agents: CellCept (mycophenolate) or other immunosuppressants
  • Thymectomy — RCT-proven benefit in patients with generalized MG who are AChR-binding antibody positive
