The Dizzy Patient

Patients use the term dizziness to describe a wide variety of symptoms: vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium, nausea, diplopia, etc., and their description often varies from moment to moment. Take the best history you can, but be at peace with the fact that most patients just can’t reliably tell you what they’re feeling. Try to sort the rest out with a good neuro exam and the special maneuvers below. If all else fails, all dizzy people over the age of 60, especially with vascular risk factors, should be fully evaluated for an acute neurovascular event.

Your task in the ED is to categorize the patient into one of three groups based on TIMING and TRIGGERS:

1 1. Acute Vestibular Syndrome

Timing: continuous (can be triggered or spontaneous)

1.1 Triggered / Traumatic Differential

Blunt head injury, anticonvulsants, aminoglycoside antibiotics, carbon monoxide

1.2 Spontaneous Differential

  • 95% either vestibular neuritis (peripheral) vs stroke (central)
  • Rarely: MS (2–3%), thiamine deficiency

1.3 History

Lasts for hours to days; central usually has associated symptoms (e.g., dysarthria, diplopia, ataxia, weakness) but not required; either can have unilateral hearing loss (peripheral = labyrinthitis, central = AICA stroke)

1.4 Distinguish Peripheral vs Central: HINTS to INFARCT

(sens 100%, spec 96%; Stroke 2009;40:3504)

Patient must be currently symptomatic for tests to be reliable.

  1. Head Impulse — ask the patient to fixate on your nose and move his/her head rapidly 30° off midline and back to center. Look for corrective saccades. Test is normal in central vertigo, abnormal with peripheral vertigo (vestibular nerve dysfunction).
  2. Nystagmus — evaluate in primary gaze and horizontal/vertical gaze in all directions. Benign etiologies have unidirectional nystagmus throughout, although the intensity may be different. Central lesions have horizontal direction change (e.g., left-beating in left gaze, right-beating in right gaze). Vertical nystagmus is always central.
  3. Test of Skew — ask the patient to fixate on your nose and then cover/uncover each eye. If positive, you will see vertical corrective saccade/refixation when moving from one eye to the other (horizontal movement doesn’t count).

Test interpretation (mnemonic: INFARCT) — Concern for stroke if one or more of the following is true:

  • Impulse Normal
  • Fast-phase nystagmus Alternating
  • Refixation on Cover Test

1.5 Plan

Central findings: Admit as a presumed posterior circulation stroke. PICA infarctions can be subtle and can evolve over time. Be careful with use of MRI to identify these patients (especially in the ED) — hyperacute MRI may appear falsely negative in posterior fossa infarction.

Peripheral findings: Fluids, meclizine, steroid taper. May need admission (to medicine preferably) if significant vomiting or unable to ambulate.

2 2. Spontaneous Episodic Vestibular Syndrome

Main differential to distinguish by history: TIA vs vestibular migraine

Duration Differential
Seconds Vestibular paroxysmia, cardiogenic
Minutes TIA, vestibular migraine, panic, hypoglycemia
Hours Vestibular migraine, Meniere’s disease, toxic/metabolic
Days Vestibular migraine

3 3. Triggered Episodic Vestibular Syndrome

History: lasts for <2 minutes, brought on by head/body movement

Main differential: BPPV vs orthostatic hypotension; central causes (rare, <10%): CPPV (lesion near 4th ventricle), Bowhunter syndrome (compression of VA with head turn)

3.1 BPPV

Dix-Hallpike may produce classic mixed upbeating and torsional nystagmus towards the affected ear that starts after a delay (10–40 seconds — this takes patience during the exam!), then goes away after 30 seconds (“fatigues”), then the patient may feel spinning in the opposite direction upon sitting up; one side is usually much worse than the other. The response is less severe if you Dix-Hallpike them again (“fatigues”).

Plan: Teach them the Epley; have them follow up with ENT if it doesn’t improve.

3.2 CPPV

Presence of additional symptoms (HA, diplopia, CN abnormalities), atypical nystagmus (downbeat, starts instantly, persists >90 seconds, unresponsive to improvement with Epley)

3.3 Bowhunter Syndrome

Symptom onset with lateral head turn; confirm with dynamic TCDs
