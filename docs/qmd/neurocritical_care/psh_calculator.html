Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity assessment measure

1 What is PSH?

  • PSH is a disorder in the regulation of autonomic function that occurs with acute brain injury, most notably severe TBI.
  • It is the overall severity of brain injury (not location) that increases risk of PSH development.
  • Mechanisms remain elusive, but the excitatory/inhibitory model (loss of central disinhibition of sympathetic and motor pathways) leading to loss of control of excitatory spinal circuits is well accepted.
  • Associated conditions: brain trauma, anoxic-brain injury, intracranial hemorrhage, autoimmune encephalitis, ischemic stroke, hydrocephalus, and cerebral fat embolism.

2 How does it present?

  • PSH is episodic, usually starts within 1–2 weeks from injury, and on average lasts 2 weeks for severe TBI patients.
  • Recurrent episodes are often induced by stimulation, and resolve spontaneously after a variable period of time or after administration of an abortive medication.
  • Core clinical features include tachycardia, hypertension, tachypnea, hyperthermia, sweating, and/or increased muscle tone with possible dystonic posturing

3 How to diagnose?

  • If you suspect PSH, the immediate next step is to rule-out mimics
  • PSH mimics: infection, pulmonary embolism, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, autonomic dysreflexia in spinal cord injury, seizures, and hydrocephalus
  • The Paroxysmal Sympathetic Hyperactivity—Assessment Measure can assist in the diagnosis of PSH, also being designed for routine daily collection. Serial collection of PSH-AM data allows clinical certainty to increase or decrease along with the evolution of the patient’s clinical status

4 How to manage PSH

  • The goals of managing PSH are to reduce pain, dampen the sympathetic system, and decrease muscle tone
  • Reduce triggers, including: urinary retention, constipation, gastric paresis, spasticity, poor positioning (where spasticity may contribute), allodynic responses to poorly fitting orthotics, pressure sores and other causes of pain, environmental factors such as ambient temperature and noise.
  • Supportive treatment (hydration, reduction of fever, correction of electrolytes, nutrition) should be optimised in parallel to pharmacological treatment for PSH
  • Sedating drugs (benzodiazepines, anesthetics) should be minimized wherever possible when treating PSH
  • Opiates are often necessary for PSH early after injury. However, should be tapered when possible at later stages (med/surg, rehab).
  • If severe symptoms persist for months, more invasive procedures can be considered, such as Intratechal Baclofen for spasticity.

Sympatholytics

- Guanfacine: PO 0.5-2mg q12h; monitor for hypotension and sedation

- Gabapentin: 600–1200mg TID PO; monitor for hypotension sedation

Spasticity/Posturing Management
- Baclofen: 5-30mg q6-q12h; monitor for sedation, constipation and hypotension

Fever
