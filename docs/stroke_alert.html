Stroke Alert

ALL ALERTS MUST BE DISCUSSED WITH STROKE ATTENDING/FELLOW IN REAL TIME

1 Time Guidelines

  • Intravenous TNK: < 4.5 hours of symptom onset (consider wake-up protocol if <4.5h)
  • Mechanical thrombectomy: potentially up to 24h depending on perfusion scan

1.1 Joint Commission Goals

  • Door to neuro eval: <15 min
  • Door to CT completion: <20 min
  • CT complete to interpretation: <20 min (use the time stamp in Visage if you interpreted in real time)
  • Door to IV TNK: <30 min
  • Door to groin puncture (thrombectomy): <60 min

2 Initial Steps

  1. Last known normal (LKN): When was the patient last known normal? Don’t trust anybody — FIND OUT YOURSELF. Obtain family phone contact information from EMS in case needed for timeline confirmation and eventual consent.
  2. Brief medical history: Similar sx in past, acuity of onset, fluctuation/progression of sx, known stroke causes (Afib, carotid disease), associated sx (headache/neck pain, altered consciousness, abnormal movements), current antithrombotics.
  3. Check blood pressure.
  4. Fingerstick glucose. Correct if <50 or >400. If still symptomatic, proceed with acute vascular workup.
  5. Rapid neuro exam/NIHSS — do NOT delay CT/IV/blood draw. Learn to work around ED providers.
  6. Take patient to CT scan as soon as possible.
    • UF stroke alert protocol: non-contrast HCT, CTA head/neck, and CT perfusion.
    • Non-contrast HCT alone is sufficient for TNK decision. If perfusion scanner is down, do NOT waste time.
    • Stay with the patient and immediately review the scan yourself.
  7. Review TNK inclusion/exclusion criteria.
  8. Call stroke attending/fellow as soon as possible.
  9. Review risks/benefits with patient/family. Obtain verbal consent if possible (see suggested language below).
  10. If TNK planned:
    • Inform nurse/pharmacist/ED team.
    • Get weight in kilograms.
    • Place order in Epic: TNK (single bolus) 0.25 mg/kg; max 25 mg (order set: ‘NEURO Stroke Thrombolytic Administration’).
    • Ensure 2 well-functioning IVs (preferably 20-gauge or larger).
    • BP must be <185/110 before pushing TNK.
    • Remain at bedside until TNK administered.
  11. Consider mechanical thrombectomy and/or acute stroke clinical trial.

Suggested consent language:
“You are having an acute stroke, caused by a blood clot blocking one of the arteries in your brain. The symptoms you’re currently having may become permanent if we don’t act quickly. You qualify for a medication called TNK, which will help dissolve the blood clot. Studies in thousands of patients with strokes like yours have shown that with this medication, patients have a much better chance of recovering to normal and living at home after a stroke. Because this medication temporarily thins your blood, there is a possible risk of bleeding, including bleeding in the brain. However, the potential benefit from the treatment is 6–10 times greater than the risks of harm, and I believe this medication is the best chance we have of reversing your symptoms. The faster we start the treatment, the better your chances of recovery. Do we have your permission to proceed?”

If BP elevated:
- Labetalol 10-20 mg IV over 1-2 minutes, may repeat ×1 OR
- Nicardipine gtt at 5 mg/hr IV, titrate by 2.5 mg/hr every 5-15 min (max 15 mg/hr). Consider clevidipine.

2.1 Tenecteplase Inclusion Criteria

  • Clinical diagnosis of stroke with disabling neurological deficits
  • Symptom onset / last seen normal within past 4.5 hours

AHA 2026 Absolute & Relative Contraindications

  • Absolute Contraindications
  • Relative Contraindications
  • No Contraindication (Generally Safe)
Condition Details
CT with extensive hypodensity IV thrombolysis should not be administered to patients whose brain imaging exhibits regions of clear hypodensity that appear to be responsible for the clinical symptoms of stroke. Clear hypodensity is when the degree of hypodensity is greater than the density of contralateral unaffected white matter.
CT with hemorrhage IV thrombolysis should not be administered to patients when CT brain imaging reveals an acute intracranial hemorrhage.
Moderate to severe traumatic brain injury <14 days For patients with AIS and a history of recent moderate to severe traumatic brain injury within 14 days that incurred >30 minutes of unconsciousness and Glasgow Coma Scale of <13 OR evidence of hemorrhage, contusion, or skull fracture on neuroimaging.
Neurosurgery <14 days For patients with AIS and a history of intracranial/spinal surgery within 14 days, IV thrombolysis is potentially harmful and should not be administered.
Acute spinal cord injury within 3 months For patients with AIS who harbor an intraspinal/intramedullary neoplasm, treatment with IV thrombolysis is potentially harmful and should not be administered.
Intracranial neoplasm For patients with AIS who harbor an intracranial neoplasm, treatment with IV thrombolysis is potentially harmful and should not be administered.
Infective endocarditis For patients with AIS and symptoms consistent with infective endocarditis, treatment with IV thrombolysis should not be administered.
Severe coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia The safety and efficacy of IV thrombolysis for AIS in patients with platelet count <100,000/mm³, INR >1.7, aPTT >40 s, or PT >15 s is unknown though may substantially increase risk of harm and should not be administered.
Aortic arch dissection For patients with AIS and known or suspected aortic arch dissection, treatment with IV thrombolysis is potentially harmful and should not be administered.
Amyloid angiopathy / CAA-ri The risk of thrombolysis-related ICH in patients on amyloid immunotherapy or with ARIA is unknown and IV thrombolysis should be avoided in such patients.
Condition Details
Pre-existing disability The benefits and risks of offering IV thrombolysis to patients with pre-existing disability and/or frailty remain uncertain. Treatment should be determined on an individualized basis.
DOAC exposure For patients taking a direct oral anticoagulant, the safety of IV thrombolysis is uncertain. In the absence of specific laboratory tests (anti-Xa or dilute thrombin time) and time of last dose is unknown or <48 h, benefit/risk discussion is required.
Major surgery or serious trauma within 14 days For patients with AIS who have undergone major surgery or serious trauma within the previous 14 days, IV thrombolysis may be considered after weighing the risk of surgical-site bleeding versus the anticipated benefit of thrombolysis.
GI or GU hemorrhage within 21 days For patients with AIS who have a history of previous GI or GU hemorrhage within 21 days, IV thrombolysis may be considered after weighing the risk of recurrent hemorrhage versus the anticipated benefit of thrombolysis.
Recent major surgery For patients with recent major surgery (within 14 days) or serious trauma, treatment with IV thrombolysis may be considered after weighing the risk of surgical-site hemorrhage versus the anticipated benefits of thrombolysis.
Prior ICH For patients with a history of previous ICH, IV thrombolysis may be considered after weighing the risk of recurrent ICH versus the anticipated benefit of thrombolysis.
Recent myocardial infarction For patients with recent MI (within 3 months), treatment with IV thrombolysis may be considered after weighing the risk of myocardial rupture versus the anticipated benefits of thrombolysis.
Seizure at onset For patients with AIS presenting with seizure at onset who have residual post-ictal deficits, IV thrombolysis may be considered if residual deficits are thought to be due to stroke rather than a post-ictal state.
Blood glucose <50 or >400 mg/dL For patients with AIS and blood glucose <50 mg/dL or >400 mg/dL, IV thrombolysis may be considered after glucose is corrected and residual deficits persist.
Pregnancy For patients with AIS who are pregnant, IV thrombolysis may be considered after careful weighing of maternal and fetal risks versus benefits.
Arterial puncture at noncompressible site within 7 days For patients with AIS who have had an arterial puncture at a noncompressible site within the previous 7 days, IV thrombolysis may be considered after weighing the risk of access-site hemorrhage versus the anticipated benefits of thrombolysis.
Large untreated aneurysm For patients with AIS and a large untreated intracranial aneurysm, the safety of IV thrombolysis is uncertain.
Condition Details
Menstruation IV thrombolysis is unlikely to be associated with clinically significant menstrual bleeding and is reasonable in otherwise eligible patients.
Extracranial cervical artery dissection IV thrombolysis in patients with AIS and extracranial cervical artery dissection is likely low risk and is reasonable.
Unruptured intracranial aneurysm IV thrombolysis in patients with AIS and unruptured intracranial aneurysm is likely low risk. Benefit likely outweighs risk.
Angiography IV thrombolysis in patients with AIS during or immediately after angiography is reasonable.
Microbleeds The risk of symptomatic ICH in patients with AIS and small numbers of cerebral microbleeds is low. IV thrombolysis is reasonable.
GI bleeding remote For patients with remote history of GI bleeding who are otherwise candidates for IV thrombolysis, treatment is reasonable.
History of stroke Patients with history of previous ischemic stroke who are otherwise candidates may still receive IV thrombolysis after individual risk-benefit discussion.
Moyamoya IV thrombolysis in patients with Moyamoya disease does not appear to pose a substantial increased risk.

3 Mechanical Thrombectomy

Consideration should begin immediately after TNK decision.

General eligibility: Large-vessel occlusion on CTA (M1, M2, A1, P1, ICA, basilar), symptom onset <24h, NIHSS ≥6, ASPECTS ≥3.

  1. Symptom onset <6h: Do NOT wait for CTP per AHA.
  2. 6-24h: Ideal = large penumbra/small core on CTP (DAWN/DEFUSE-3); benefit even with large core (ASPECTS ≥3).
  3. Hyperacute MRI/MRA if contrast issue (discuss with neurorads).
  • Review LVO <24h LKW with stroke fellow/attending.
  • Stroke attending calls endovascular neurosurgery.
  • Call ShandsCair (#50300) or #69 for endovascular alert.
  • Call neurosurgery consult resident (352-283-5709) and IR tech (352-256-5402).
  • Accompany patient to OR for signout.
  • Place admission orders ASAP.
  • Accompany to NICU for post-procedure NIHSS.

4 Post-TNK / Post-Thrombectomy Orders

(use order set “NEURO Stroke POST Thrombolytic Administration”)

  • Admit to NICU (call CCM fellow)
  • BP monitoring: maintain <180/105
    • Vitals q15 min ×2h, then q30 min ×6h, then q1h ×16h
  • No heparin, warfarin, aspirin, or other antithrombotics for 24h if TNK given
  • Avoid invasive procedures (NG, Foley, non-compressible arterial access) for 24h if TNK
  • Repeat CT head 24h post-TNK — if negative, start ASA 325 mg and DVT prophylaxis

5 Complications of TNK

Intracranial hemorrhage and angioedema

5.1 Angioedema

  • Assess airway immediately
  • Epinephrine 0.5 mL IM + Benadryl 25 mg IV

5.2 Suspected Intracranial Hemorrhage

  • Worsening LOC, new/worsening neuro deficits, nausea/vomiting, severe headache, Cushing response, seizure
  • STAT CT head
  • CALL ATTENDING/FELLOW IMMEDIATELY
  • Labs: CBC, PT/PTT, platelets, fibrinogen, D-dimer
  • Tighten BP: SBP 100-140 (nicardipine infusion)
  • Reversal: Cryoprecipitate 10 units IV + TXA 1 g IV (if indicated)
  • Obtain TEG/fibrinogen 2h post-reversal; repeat cryo if fibrinogen <150
  • Refractory/life-threatening bleed: consider aminocaproic acid

Always discuss management with stroke attending/fellow.
