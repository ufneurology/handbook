Diagnosis and Management of Delirium

Agitation happens for a variety of reasons in the inpatient setting including psychosis, toxidromes, delirium, substance withdrawal, or rational people deciding they no longer want to conform to hospital restrictions. With agitation, it is always advisable to back off patients if you feel unsafe, involve other staff, and call security if the situation escalates. Restraints, both chemical and physical, are often necessary both for preventing patient and staff harm.

General Strategies
  1. Avoid/remove aggravating factors
  2. Identify/treat cause
  3. Supportive care
  4. Control dangerous/disruptive behavior
Prevention
  • Orientation protocol, structured activities, reassurance, sitter (ideally family)
  • Visual/hearing aids
  • Nonpharmacological sleep aids, maintain day/night cycle (quiet time at night)
  • Pain management, early mobilization

Avoid: frequent change of environment (e.g., room changes), restraints, constipation, anticholinergics, catheters

1 How to find causes?

1.1 History

  • Onset
  • Precipitating factor
  • Baseline level of functioning (history of dementia?)
  • Recent infectious symptoms (dysuria, nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, cough)
  • Symptoms of CNS infection (neck stiffness, headache, nausea)
  • Access to toxic ingestion or illicit substances including alcohol
  • Associated focal deficits or abnormal movements

1.2 Exam

Full general exam with attention to signs of intoxication, dehydration and infection; neurologic exam including MMSE with close attention to level of consciousness, attentiveness, and focal deficits. Check and document meningeal signs, asterixis and subtle signs of seizure.

1.3 Differential

  • Drugs (most common, illicit or prescribed)
  • Endocrine, Electrolytes, Ethanol, Emotional, Eyes/Ears
  • Low O₂, Lack of drugs, Liver
  • Infection (second most common, especially in elderly)
  • Retention
  • Intracranial (stroke/hemorrhage/mass)
  • Uremia, Under-nutrition, Under-hydration
  • Metabolic
Tip

Often two hits:

  1. Baseline brain disease (stroke/PD/dementia), advanced age, sensory impairment (absence of hearing aid/glasses), psychiatric illness, pain, absence of orienting devices (e.g., clock)

PLUS

  1. Systemic/neurological issues (toxin, infection, metabolic derangement, etc.)

1.4 Workup

Determined by above but typically includes:

  • Routine labs; consider TSH, vitamin B12, VDRL, ammonia, thiamine
    • Look for: hyper/hypoglycemia, hyper/hypoNa⁺, hyperCa⁺⁺, uremia, hepatic enceph, hypercarbia, hypothyroidism
  • Urine toxicology screen and alcohol level; review medication list for precipitants
    • Benzos, opiates, antihistamines, anticholinergics, neuroleptics, DA agonists, AEDs, lithium, TCAs, theophylline, digoxin, steroids, amantadine, H2 blockers, anti-emetics, INH, salicylates, FK506, quinolones, etc.
  • Urinalysis, culture, and chest x-ray
  • Non-contrast CT head ± CTA; MRI for focal deficits
    • Consider diffuse cortical hypoperfusion or infarcts, R parietal infarcts, bilateral thalamic infarcts (top-of-the-basilar syndrome), brainstem infarct; edema/mass effect secondary to stroke/tumor, carcinomatous meningitis, SDH
  • ± LP if fever, leukocytosis, meningeal signs/symptoms, or any concern for HSV encephalitis
  • EEG to rule-out seizures or non-convulsive status and assess degree of encephalopathy

1.5 Pharmacologic Interventions

Pharmacologic interventions have varying efficacy based on etiology; in general the following apply:

1.6 Delirious patients (decreased sensorium, poor attention)

Benzodiazepines and anticholinergic medications should be avoided. Antipsychotics should be first line.

  • Often low-dose haloperidol (0.5 to 2 mg) IM, PO, or IV can be helpful.
  • Crude guideline: monitor response to the first dose and treat accordingly.
    • If improvement is seen → consider halving the dose
    • If no change → may repeat the dose after some interval (1–2 hrs)
    • If the situation worsens → may double the dose
  • Generally accepted max effective dose per 24 hr interval is around 15 mg
  • Watch for QT prolongation by monitoring with EKG once the patient is compliant
  • Common management: standing haldol in delirium, 0.5–1 mg Q12 (frequently used and evidence-based, albeit weakly)
Extreme agitation

Where the potential for mortal self-harm is possible, benzodiazepines at high doses may be necessary, to the point of requiring intubation.

1.7 Withdrawal

For individuals thought to be in sedative withdrawal or with acute toxidromes, including cocaine intoxication, benzodiazepines are first line for agitation.

Features suggestive of withdrawal: tremor, diaphoresis, self-reported craving, dysautonomia, history of daily RTC alcohol or benzodiazepine use, history of withdrawal.

  • Ativan is a good choice (available IM, IV, and PO)
  • Generally treat in 1–2 mg doses with monitoring at fixed intervals

1.8 Individuals without delirium or withdrawal

An effective and safe strategy is a combination of low-dose haldol, ativan, and benadryl:

  • Common combination: haldol 2 mg + ativan 2 mg + benadryl 25 or 50 mg
  • Can be drawn together and given in one syringe if necessary
  • Can be given in stages if people are not responding after the first dose
  • Onset of action is similar for oral and IM preparations → patient cooperation decides route

1.9 Drug-induced extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS)

  • Quetiapine (Seroquel) has a lower affinity for the D2 receptor and results in fewer EPS symptoms than haloperidol
  • Some evidence for its use in the treatment of delirium
  • Use in acute agitation limited by unavailability in parenteral forms

2 Rapidly Progressive Dementia Workup

These can be some of the most complicated cases you will see in the hospital and you will often exhaust every test imaginable searching for an etiology, but here is a basic “starter kit” to work off of:

  1. Serologic studies: CBC, Comprehensive metabolic panel (including Ca, Mg, and PO₄), liver panel, ammonia, RPR, ESR, CRP, ANA, TSH, Free T4, vitamin B₁₂, Lyme antibody, and HIV
  2. Urinalysis
  3. CSF analysis: cell count and differential, protein, glucose, VDRL, immunoglobulin G (IgG) index, and oligoclonal bands
  4. MRI brain with/without Gad
  5. EEG
