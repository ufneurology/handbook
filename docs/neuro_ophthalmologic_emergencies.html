Neuro-ophthalmologic Emergencies

1 Acute Third Nerve Palsy

  • Start with a HCT to rule out subarachnoid blood or herniation.
  • Complete or partial ophthalmoparesis with pupil involvement requires some type of angiography to evaluate for a compressive aneurysm — start with CTA or MRA but then may still need conventional angiogram. You may also want to get MRI instead of / in addition to CT scan depending on luxuries of time and resource.
  • Complete ophthalmoparesis with spared pupil in patients over 50 with vascular risk factors is likely ischemic palsy; consider getting an MRI scan and then observing. Usually improve within 2–3 months.
  • Partial ophthalmoparesis with spared pupil could be caused by an aneurysm; angiography is needed.
  • If in doubt with an acute 3rd nerve palsy, image and obtain angiography.

2 Pituitary Apoplexy

Symptoms: Classic syndrome — HA, sudden visual loss (both visual field and acuity), and ophthalmoplegia. May be accompanied by meningeal signs, hypopituitarism, unstable BP, encephalopathy, facial pain/numbness.

Differential: Pituitary tumor infarction or hemorrhage and post-partum hemorrhage / Sheehan syndrome.

Management:

  • CT scan may miss so MRI (pituitary-protocol) is needed.
  • LP may demonstrate subarachnoid blood and aseptic meningitis.
  • If MRI isn’t conclusive and there is SAH, you may have to get angiographic study to rule out aneurysm.
  • Immediate therapy includes steroids / hydrocortisone to prevent shock from acute hypopituitarism.
  • Electrolyte abnormalities may be prominent and need to be addressed acutely.

3 Giant Cell Arteritis

Symptoms: HA, tenderness of scalp, temple or face of affected side(s), jaw claudication and current or past history of polymyalgia rheumatica, acute vision loss — anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (AION); can also have central retinal artery occlusion, posterior circulation infarction. Usually female (ratio 3:1) and over age 50 (almost unheard of <50 yrs). May have premonitory symptoms of transient monocular blindness, bright light amaurosis, transient binocular diplopia and formed visual hallucinations.

Exam: Look for scalp tenderness, visual acuity loss, chalky white disc edema, and cotton wool spots. If evidence of both central retinal artery occlusion and AION, you have likely sealed the diagnosis. Can have motility defects from ischemia of the extra-ocular muscles, nerves or a brainstem infarct.

Management:

  • Check ESR — most will be above 40 mm/hr in giant cell arteritis.
  • Consult vascular / ophtho for biopsy.
  • Immediately treat with steroids — IV Solumedrol 1 g daily × 3–5 days then 1 mg/kg per day PO prednisone + Tocilizumab (Rheum will arrange).
Warning

Do not wait for biopsy to start treatment!

4 Ocular Cranial Nerve Palsies: General Rules for Localization

  • Diplopia resolves upon covering one eye: if it doesn’t the problem is likely ocular or refractive.
  • On cover-uncover testing, an eye that is deviated out (exotropic) will move in when uncovered to pick up the fixation target (usually a finger or your nose) → Think INO, IIIrd n. palsy or exotropia related to medication effect.
  • If the eyes move out with uncover (esotropia) → think of a sixth nerve palsy or abduction deficit.
  • Mimics of extraocular nerve palsies: thyroid eye disease, myasthenia gravis, internuclear ophthalmoplegia, decompensated phoria, orbital wall fractures, Wernicke’s syndrome, skew deviation, botulism, orbital tumors, Miller Fisher variant of Guillain-Barré Syndrome, mitochondrial d/o (CPEO: chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia, Kearns-Sayre Syndrome), vitamin E deficiency

5 Afferent Visual Complaints

  1. History: monocular vs. binocular, pain, timing
  2. Exam: acuity (best corrected with glasses and pinhole), color (especially red), visual fields (including blind spot), fundus, pupils (is there an APD)
  3. Special tests: visual fields (Humphrey, Goldmann), OCT (quantifies retinal nerve fiber layer thickness), orbital ultrasound and the rest of the neurology favorites (MRI, LP etc.)
