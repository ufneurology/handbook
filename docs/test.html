Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage

1 Epidemiology

  • ~3% of all strokes
  • Incidence ≈ 3 per 100,000
  • ~30% 90-day mortality among hospitalized patients
  • Mean age at presentation ≈ 55 years, younger than other stroke patients leading to more years of life lost due to disability
  • Patients with ≥2 first-degree relatives with aneurysms have ~12% higher likelihood of having an aneurysm
  • Posterior circulation aneurysms have the highest mortality.

2 Initial assessment

Most common symptom of aneurysmal SAH is headache. Neck stiffness/pain is an important part of the history and is built into the Ottawa SAH rule. Ictus (time of headache onset) is critical for work-up and management.

Thunderclap headache: specific pattern of severe headaches that reaches maximum intensity usually in less than one minute, but up to 1 hour.

2.1 Thunderclap headache differential

Pattern Differential
Thunderclap headache aSAH, pituitary apoplexy, arterial dissection, CVT/DVST, hypertensive emergency, RCVS, intracranial hypotension, primary thunderclap HA
Tip

Apply the Ottawa SAH rule to rule out clinically (close to 100% sensitiivty).

Imaging required if the patient has ≥1 of:

  • Age >40 years of age

  • Thunderclap headache

  • Neck pain / stiffness

  • Limited neck flexion on exam

  • Onset during exertion

  • Witnessed loss of consciousness

    If all of the above are absent, patient can be discharged without further work-up

3 Work-up

  • Ictus <6 h from presentation AND no new neurological deficits: non-contrast CT head is sufficient to rule out SAH if the scan is read by a fellowship-trained neuroradiologist (NPV approaches 100%).
  • Ictus >6 h OR new neurological deficits: CT head → if negative, LP looking for xanthochromia which may not be detectable in the first 6 hours after ictus.

4 Grading

Grading helps predict delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) and future functional outcome.

4.1 Exam-based: Hunt and Hess

Grade Description
0 Unruptured aneurysm
1 Asymptomatic or mild HA and nuchal rigidity
1a No meningeal signs but fixed neurologic deficit
2 CN palsy, moderate/severe HA, nuchal rigidity
3 Mild focal deficit, confusion or drowsiness
4 Stupor, moderate/severe hemiparesis
5 Deep coma, decerebrate posturing, moribund appearance

4.2 Imaging-based: Modified Fisher Scale

  • Grade 1
  • Grade 2
  • Grade 3
  • Grade 4

Thin focal or diffuse SAH · No IVH

  • Thin = <1 mm
  • Symptomatic vasospasm risk: ≈ 24%

Thin focal or diffuse SAH · With IVH

  • Thin SAH + any IVH
  • Symptomatic vasospasm risk: ≈ 33%

Thick focal or diffuse SAH · No IVH

  • Thick = ≥1 mm (or completely filling ≥1 cistern/fissure)
  • Symptomatic vasospasm risk: ≈ 33%

Thick focal or diffuse SAH · With IVH

  • Thick SAH + any IVH
  • Symptomatic vasospasm risk: ≈ 40% (highest)
Warning

“Irreversible injury” / “unsalvageable” can be labeled only after correction of seizures, hydrocephalus, hyponatremia, and hypothermia.

Absent brainstem reflexes on arrival must be rechecked after those corrections and again at 24 h.

Futility may be considered if:

  • Persistently absent brainstem reflexes
  • No purposeful response to pain
  • Large infarct on initial CT
  • Global cerebral edema

5 Immediate management

Primary goal: secure the aneurysm to prevent rebleeding.

  • Ideal: within 24 h
  • Acceptable: 24–72 h
  • Admit to a NeuroICU. High-volume units with expert nursing reduce poor functional outcomes — serial exam remains paramount for DCI diagnosis.

Discuss surgical options with neurosurgery.

Dicussing of aneurysm securament with Neurosurgery
  • Coiling is usually preferred when anatomically feasible, it is associated with better 1-year functional outcomes.
  • Clipping should be considered for aneurysms at high risk of rerupture and may be preferred in patients <40 years because of greater durability (coiled aneurysms have a higher late rerupture rate).
  • High-density stents / flow diverters (e.g. Pipeline) for wide-neck, fusiform, or blister aneurysms not amenable to coiling or clipping.
  • Stents and flow diverters require DAPT, which raises bleeding risk — use only when there is no other viable option.

Per guidelines, aneurysm securament is still recommended in high modified Fisher grades and in older patients. The only situation without proven benefit is irreversible neurological injury.

6 Early management (first 72 hours)

6.1 Rebleeding

  • Occurs in ~5% of patients within the first 24 h.
  • Reduce BP variability; avoid both hypo- and hypertension.
  • No single data-driven SBP target; a reasonable pre-secure goal is SBP 140–160 mmHg, reduced gradually.
  • Level 1 / expert opinion: reverse anticoagulation.
Note

What about TXA?

The ULTRA trial showed no outcome benefit. Tranexamic acid has therefore largely fallen out of use.

6.2 Hydrocephalus

  • Can be early or late.
  • More common with IVH, but can occur with no visible intraventricular blood.
  • Clinical clues: AMS or restricted upward gaze (tectal compression from third-ventricle dilation).
  • Treatment: CSF drainage (EVD vs lumbar drain).

6.3 Seizures

  • Can occur at any time (early or late).
  • Do not use routine seizure prophylaxis except in high-risk patients.
  • No guideline indication for prophylaxis. If seizures are documented, treat for at least 7 days.

6.4 Elevated ICP

Consider ICP monitoring if:

  • GCS <8

  • Neurological deterioration

  • Hydrocephalus

  • Cerebral edema

7 Late Managment

Most important is Delayed cerberal ishcemia

7.1 Delayed cerberal ishcemia

  • Occurs in ~30% of patients, mainly days 4–14 from ictus.
  • Leading cause of disability among survivors.
  • Diagnosis is elusive: classically tied to angiographic spasm, but small-vessel spasm may be invisible to current imaging.
Important

Three interventions prevent DCI:

1. Enteral nimodipine (Class 1A)

2. Euvolemia

3. Avoidance of hypotension (MAP > 65), hypertension (SBP < 160) and minimizing blood pressure variability

7.1.1 Monitoring

Serial clinical exam remains the best tool. Complementary tests are most useful in high-grade aSAH, coma, or a fluctuating exam.

Modality Role / performance Recommendation
Clinical exam Best first-line diagnosis of DCI Standard of care
DSA Gold standard for angiographic vasospasm Reference standard
CTA Sensitivity ~82%, specificity ~97% 2a / Class B
CT perfusion (CTP) Detects tissue-level hypoperfusion Adjunct
TCD Screening for large-vessel spasm 2a / Class B
EEG Useful in comatose / high-grade patients 2a / Class B
PbtO2, lactate/pyruvate ratio Multimodal neuromonitoring 2b / Class B

7.1.1.1 TCD interpretation (MCA)

Common thresholds used in practice (always interpret with the exam and other imaging):

Finding Interpretation
Mean flow velocity (MFV) <120 cm/s Unlikely significant MCA spasm
MFV 120–200 cm/s Possible / moderate spasm — correlate clinically
MFV >200 cm/s High likelihood of severe MCA spasm
Lindegaard ratio (MCA MFV / extracranial ICA) <3 Hyperemia more likely than spasm
Lindegaard ratio 3–6 Mild–moderate spasm
Lindegaard ratio >6 Severe spasm
Rapid rise in MFV day-to-day Concerning even before absolute cutoffs

TCD is operator-dependent, limited for posterior circulation and distal vessels, and can miss small-vessel DCI.

7.1.1.2 EEG ratios

In high-grade or comatose patients, continuous EEG can flag DCI before a clinical change:

  • Rising alpha/delta ratio (ADR) deterioration or loss of reactivity is concerning.
  • Focal slowing or new epileptiform activity may accompany ischemia or seizure.
  • Quantitative EEG trends (ADR, relative alpha variability) are more useful than isolated snapshots.

7.1.1.3 CT perfusion

  • Prolonged MTT or Tmax with relatively preserved CBV suggests salvageable hypoperfusion (treatable DCI) rather than completed infarct.
  • Use as an adjunct when exam is unreliable, not as a standalone rule-in/rule-out.

7.1.2 Treatment of DCI

  • Induced hypertension is first-line
  • Endovascular rescue (IA vasodilators such as verapamil or milrinone, angioplasty) may be considered if induced hypertension fails.

7.2 Long-term

Screening for depression, anxiety, and sexual dysfunction improves long-term outcomes.
